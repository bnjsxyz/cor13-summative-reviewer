Lesson 1 • Introduction to Philosophy

Meaning and purpose

  • Philosophy comes from philos (love) and sophia (wisdom). It examines fundamental questions using critical and logical reasoning.
  • Philosophy functions as a discipline, a framework for examining beliefs and assumptions, and an examination of knowledge.
  • People philosophize because of wonder (Plato), doubt (Descartes), challenging experiences or limit situations (Jaspers), and the continuing love of wisdom.
AreaCentral concernTypical question
LogicValid and sound reasoningDoes the conclusion follow from the reasons?
EpistemologyKnowledge and justificationHow do we know?
MetaphysicsReality, being, and existenceWhat is ultimately real?
EthicsRight action and the goodWhat ought we to do?
Political philosophyJustice, rights, governmentWhat makes authority just?
AestheticsBeauty, art, and tasteWhat makes something beautiful?
Exam lens: Identify the branch from the main question being asked. A situation may contain several details, but locate its central concern.
Lesson 2 • Doing Philosophy
ConceptMeaningClue in a situation
Partial point of viewExamines one component or only some sides of a whole.A conclusion rests on one interest, one source, or incomplete information.
Holistic point of viewRelates the relevant parts and contexts to understand the whole.Several effects, stakeholders, and kinds of evidence are connected before deciding.
Inductive reasoningMoves from specific observations or cases toward a probable general conclusion.“Several observed cases showed X; therefore X will probably occur.”
Deductive reasoningApplies a general premise or rule to reach a necessary specific conclusion when the premises are true and the form is valid.“All A are B; this is A; therefore this is B.”

Holistic and partial views are complementary. Specific details support a broad understanding, while a broad understanding helps us interpret new details. Holistic thinking does not mean treating every factor as equally important; it means considering what is relevant and weighing it reasonably.

Exam lens: Perspective asks how much of the situation is considered. Reasoning asks how the conclusion is reached. Do not confuse “partial” with “inductive” or “holistic” with “deductive.”
Lesson 3 • Methods and Tools for Philosophizing
MethodPurpose and process
Socratic methodUses probing, open-ended questions to clarify concepts, uncover assumptions, and test inconsistencies.
Dialectic methodPlaces an initial position or thesis in reasoned tension with an antithesis, allowing a more adequate synthesis.
PhenomenologyDescribes first-person lived experience. Bracketing or epoché suspends prior judgments; eidetic reduction seeks essential features.
HermeneuticsInterprets texts, symbols, or expressions through language, historical setting, culture, audience, and the relationship between the parts and the whole.
Exam lens: Questions that expose assumptions point to Socratic inquiry. Opposing arguments reaching a synthesis point to dialectic. Lived experience points to phenomenology. Meaning in context points to hermeneutics.
Lesson 4 • Determining Truth

A fact can be checked using adequate evidence. An opinion expresses a personal judgment, belief, feeling, or preference. A confident statement does not become a fact merely because many people repeat it.

TheoryA claim is supported when…Question to ask
CorrespondenceIt matches relevant facts or external reality.What observation, record, or measurement verifies it?
CoherenceIt fits consistently within an established system of beliefs or findings.Does it contradict other accepted claims in the system?
PragmaticIt proves useful or effective through tested practical consequences.Does it work reliably in practice?
ConsensusIt gains informed agreement after competent participants examine reasons and evidence.Was the agreement informed and reached through responsible inquiry?
Exam lens: Consensus is not mere popularity. Correspondence focuses on matching reality, coherence on consistency, and pragmatism on tested results.
Lesson 5 • Embodied Spirit, Limitations, and Transcendence

The human person as embodied spirit

The body and spiritual dimension form the concrete human person. Biological, psychological, economic, and theological perspectives highlight real features, but no single perspective fully exhausts personhood.

ThinkerView of body and soul
PlatoDualistic emphasis: the immaterial soul can exist apart from the mutable body. The soul includes reason, spirit or will, and desire or appetite.
AristotleHylomorphic unity: the soul is the form or actuality of the living body. Vegetative, sensitive, and rational capacities culminate in human reason and will; flourishing is eudaimonia.
St. AugustineA mortal body and immortal soul created by God, with free will and fulfillment ultimately directed toward God.
St. Thomas AquinasThe person is a body-soul unity. The rational soul is the form of the body and has intellectual and spiritual capacities.

Limitations and transcendence

  • Facticity: given conditions such as physical traits, past events, birthplace, and historical circumstances.
  • Spatial limitation: a person occupies a particular location and perceives from a situated viewpoint.
  • Temporal limitation: human life unfolds in finite time; two events at the same time may force a choice.
  • Body as intermediary: the body makes perception and expression possible, yet it may not perfectly communicate inner thoughts or intentions.
  • Transcendence: recognizing a limit and opening meaningful possibilities through attitude, choice, creativity, learning, relationship, or adaptation. It does not erase every limit.
Exam lens: First identify the limitation actually shown. Then ask whether the person denies it or responds creatively and responsibly. The second response demonstrates transcendence.
